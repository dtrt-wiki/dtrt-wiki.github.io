Strike-Face

Origin

Before the Corps perfected kinetic-energy manipulation, the technology began as a simple ceramic trauma plate manufactured by an early, defunct iteration of what was then K Corp. It is unrelated to the present-day K Corp, which produces the Heliapoeisis healing drug and has no connection to kinetic-energy technology. The plates were stamped with a directional arrow and two words on the strike side: “STRIKE ← FACE”: telling the wearer which side took the bullet. Soldiers in the field began calling them “Strike-Face plates,” then simply “Strike-Faces,” and the name outlived the plate itself.

As Former K Corp refined its understanding of kinetic energy, covering absorption, redirection and dissipation, the technology miniaturized. The ceramic plate was no longer the vessel; the principle could be embedded into rings, chains, fabric, even tattoos. But the linguistic split was already complete.

The Split

At some point in the late corporate era, R&D formally divided the product line:

BranchDomainClean NameDegraded (Backstreets)
StrikeOffensive: amplifying, directing, or delivering kinetic forceStrike Gear → StrikewearStrykewer → Stryke
FaceDefensive: absorbing, dispersing, or nullifying incoming kinetic forceFace Gear → FacewearFaysewer → Fayse

The original plates became obsolete as a corporate product but never disappeared. They were too reliable to abandon.

Operational Tiers

Modern Strike-Face accessories fall into three functional categories per branch, regardless of form factor.

Fayses: electrical management

Defensive accessories themed around electrical circuit management: current, threshold, and load.

Fuse

Sacrificial. Single-impact. Destroyed on activation.

A Fuse detects incoming kinetic energy above a calibrated threshold, absorbs the full force, and shatters irreversibly. The wearer takes nothing. The accessory is gone.

  • Analogue: ceramic trauma plate
  • Cost: cheap, disposable, easily scavenged
  • Common forms: rings, thin chains, single-use plates

Breaker

Trips and resets. Absorbs a hit, goes dormant, repairable.

A Breaker thresholds similarly to a Fuse, but rather than destroying itself, it trips, dumping the energy and entering a cooldown state. It must be manually reset, repaired, or allowed to recharge before the next activation. While dormant, it offers no protection.

  • Analogue: steel plate, takes several rounds, then needs reworking
  • Cost: mid-tier, requires a Tuner or workshop for maintenance
  • Common forms: heavier chains, reinforced bracers, plate carriers with reset switches

Buffer

Always active. Dampens impact. Near-indestructible.

A Buffer does not wait for a threshold. It is a persistent kinetic dampener, absorptive material that bleeds force out of every impact. It lets damage through; it merely ensures the damage is never fatal in a single strike. Buffers are the most durable tier, and the most expensive.

  • Analogue: UHMWPE polymer plate, catches round after round, never shatters, eventually needs replacement
  • Cost: expensive, specialist
  • Common forms: suit-integrated linings, vest inserts, high-end bracers

Strykes: thermal management

Offensive accessories themed around industrial thermal control: heat buildup, venting, and dissipation. These are heat exchangers, driven by industrial thermal control.

Flash

Unregulated thermal dump. Single-strike burnout.

A Flash converts stored kinetic potential into raw force with no thermal regulation, maximum amplification, all heat vented in one strike. Overheats the accessory, burns the user, and often destroys itself. The visible spectacle is part of the design.

  • Analogue: ceramic plate shattering on impact
  • Cost: cheap, dramatic, easy to scavenge but dangerous to use
  • Common forms: rings (Burr’s overheated bronze ring), single-use knuckles
  • Favored by: Stray Dogs, for whom every punch is a statement

Flare

Thermal spike with cooldown cycle. Burst-fire.

A Flare delivers force in discrete thermal spikes, then cycles down to shed heat before the next activation. Hits hard, but the gap between bursts leaves the user vulnerable. More controlled than a Flash, still visibly aggressive.

  • Analogue: a temperature spike on a monitoring graph, sharp rise, sharp fall
  • Cost: mid-tier, requires venting period or external cooling between cycles
  • Common forms: heavier rings, gauntlet inserts, paired accessories for staggered cycling

Sink

Continuous heat dissipation. Always on. Never overheats.

A Sink converts kinetic force to thermal energy and disperses it steadily across its surface, no spikes, no venting, no burnout. It does not amplify as aggressively as a Flash or Flare, but it never hurts the user, never quits, and never needs cycling. It is the choice most Fixers make.

  • Analogue: heatsink, constant thermal load, constant dissipation
  • Cost: expensive, specialist, requires precision engineering
  • Common forms: suit-integrated panels, gauntlet plates, professional-grade rings
  • Favored by: Fixers, a working arm is worth more than a spectacular punch

Generational Layering

TierWhat They WearWho
OriginalActual Strike-Face ceramic plates, legacy carriersWar veterans, old-timers, traditionalists. No fancy failure modes.
StreetStrykes and Fayses in scavenged form factors, stolen rings, jury-rigged chainsScavengers, low-rank gang members. Whatever could be looted.
ProfessionalPlate carriers with embedded Fayses; suit-integrated StrykesFixers, Syndicate enforcers, security contractors. The plate still catches the round when the tech fails.
FactionSame tech, factional housingStray Dogs use chains and rings for visibility; Syndicate weaves it into tailored suits; zealots tattoo circuits directly into skin.

The plate carrier persists at the professional tier for practical reasons. When the kinetic layer fails, and the Fuse shatters or the Breaker trips, there is still plain ceramic underneath. Redundancy is treated as standard practice in The City.

Vernacular

Modern Backstreets speech has long abandoned “Strike Gear” and “Face Gear”:

  • “He’s running double Fayses” — two defensive accessories stacked.
  • “That punch had a Stryke behind it” — offensive kinetic assist.
  • “Fayse popped” / “Fayse is down” — a Fuse or Breaker just triggered.
  • “Old man’s still wearing plates, swear to god, a Strykeface carrier from the war” — an antique, the technology in its original form factor.
  • “Needs a re-Fayse” — needs a replacement defensive accessory.
  • “He’s Flash-fried his arm” — overheated a Stryke and burned himself.
  • “Running hot” — a Flare mid-cycle, or a Sink approaching its thermal limit.
